## Key Insights Summary

### 1. "Say" > "Know" (Opposite of Marjorie)
Jason strongly and consistently preferred **verbatim text** over **informational hints** — the exact opposite of Marjorie's preference. His reasoning: in a conversation, there isn't enough time to read information, process it, formulate a response, and deliver it. With "say" mode, he can glance at the text, decide if it's relevant, and either say it (adapting wording on the fly) or ignore it. This is a faster cognitive loop.

> Key quote: "There's no way I can read what the AI recommended to me, decide, think about something, and then say — that's too much." [48:51-49:03]

### 2. "Outsourcing the Thinking" — Embracing AI Dependency
Jason explicitly wants to **offload cognitive labor** to the AI during conversations. He frames this positively: "I can dissociate from the conversation" while the AI handles the strategic thinking. This is a fundamentally different relationship with the technology than Marjorie, who wanted to stay in control.

### 3. Conflict Resolution is the Sweet Spot
S2 (One-on-One) received the highest ratings (H:4, A:5). In high-stakes interpersonal negotiations, Jason found the agent genuinely useful for providing "techniques to immediately resolve the tension." The AI serves as a real-time coach for difficult conversations.

### 4. Redundancy is Still #1 Complaint (Same as Marjorie)
Both participants independently identified repetitive triggering as the primary annoyance. Jason's nuance: **frequency is fine if every trigger is novel and useful.** The problem isn't how often the agent speaks — it's that it says the same thing in different words.

> "If it's not redundant, would it be fine for it to trigger that much? Yes." [58:10-58:16]

### 5. Topic Tracking Degrades Over Time
In S3, the agent's relevance deteriorated as the conversation shifted topics. It continued recommending baseball-related things when the conversation had moved to war and careers. This suggests the model's context window or prompt structure loses track of topic shifts mid-session.

### 6. "Awkward Silence" as Intervention Trigger
Jason wanted the agent to help during S5 (elevator small talk) — a scenario explicitly designed for no intervention. He saw value in having the AI generate conversation starters when both parties are struggling. This challenges the assumption that low-stakes conversations don't need help.

### 7. Brevity is Critical for Glanceable Display
Jason repeatedly complained about text length. On smart glasses, the display is glanceable — recommendations need to be short enough to read in 1-2 seconds. Full sentences to say are fine; explanations of what to know are too long.

### 8. Goal Inference Remains Accurate
Like Marjorie, Jason rated goal understanding at 4 across all scenarios. Task A is the strongest component. The failures are in Tasks B (when to intervene) and C (what to recommend).

### 9. Agent as Word-Slip Recovery
An unanticipated use case: the agent caught when Jason misspoke ("slack" instead of the intended word) and provided a correction he could naturally incorporate. This positions the agent as a real-time proofreader for spoken conversation.
